When I told people I was going back to school for an MBA, the assumption was always the same: pivot story, scientist getting tired of the bench, new LinkedIn header incoming. That's not quite what happened. What actually happened is closer to something I've written elsewhere: the lab is fun, but it isn't the whole story. I'd watched a deal I cared about get derailed by forces that had nothing to do with the underlying chemistry — regulatory posture, internal politics, the ordinary mess of people deciding what a company does next. I didn't want out of science. I wanted in on the part of the decision the science alone doesn't make. One year in, I can still remember exactly how strange that ambition felt on day one, sitting in a room full of people who'd spent careers in finance and operations, having spent mine solving protein structures almost no one outside a narrow field had ever heard of.

Nobody warns you how much of a business degree is just discomfort, scheduled. In one term I underwrote an Airbus aircraft program with nothing but a discount rate and a set of assumptions I had to defend out loud. In another, I built a leveraged buyout model for a company I'd never worked in and couldn't picture on a factory floor. I valued a newspaper — a newspaper, an asset class in visible decline — because Warren Buffett once bought one and the case wanted to know if he'd been right to. None of it had a mechanism of action to fall back on. No crystal structure to check my answer against, no gel to run overnight while I waited to see if the hypothesis held. Just the framework, and whatever judgment I'd built up by that point.

The instinct that did carry over, it turns out, is skepticism toward assumptions nobody in the room is still questioning. The clearest example was a case on Eaton's divestiture of its hydraulics division to Danfoss, where the standard approach blends a single weighted cost of capital across a business that, post-transaction, no longer shares one balance sheet. I came out of that case more skeptical of blended WACC assumptions than I went in — not because I have some innate gift for corporate finance, but because "what is actually being measured here" is the same question I used to ask about an enzyme kinetics assay, aimed at a different subject. The habit didn't need translating. It just needed somewhere new to point.

The moment that mattered more than any graded case wasn't graded at all. My team was retained by KCRW, the Los Angeles public radio station, to help redesign its membership model for an audience that increasingly finds the station through podcasts rather than a pledge drive. We built a tiered proposal, presented it, and got told — correctly — that we'd designed a pricing play and called it a product, that we'd written the value proposition from KCRW's perspective instead of the listener's, and that a research slide had used p-values as a stand-in for effect size when it should have reported the actual coefficients. I could argue with how the grading treated a deck read in isolation from the live presentation that explained it. I couldn't argue with the substance of any of those three notes, and rebuilding the deck around the listener instead of the institution changed it enough that "night and day" is the only honest description. A case study hands you a grade and moves on. A client hands you the actual consequences of being wrong, with no professor there to soften the landing. That's the difference between studying the work and doing it.

What I'm carrying into year two isn't a framework — it's the relationships I've built with people who have never worked in biotech and never will, and the instinct to be in a room before I technically need to be there, rather than waiting for an invitation that assumes I already belong. In October, my cohort travels to Vietnam and Thailand, and for the first time this isn't a slide deck about market entry — it's the actual market, with regulatory and distribution realities that don't care how clean the framework looked in class. That trip is where I find out whether a year of discomfort built anything durable, or just made me comfortable with a different kind of discomfort.

Either way, the same sentence keeps proving itself true, one case at a time: the discipline of asking the right question travels further than the subject matter it started in. I didn't believe that a year ago. I'm starting to.
