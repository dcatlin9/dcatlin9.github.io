A couple of weeks ago I wrote about a Saturday afternoon spent arguing over CAGE distance with three teammates and a deck on Abbott's pharmaceutical business in Vietnam. That deck is done. This is the next one, and it's a different company, a different team, and — I'd argue — a harder question, because this time nobody in the room had a pharma background to lean on.

Our ExPORT team, officially Team 5, skews toward military and healthcare: a logistics officer, a clinical operations veteran, me with a decade of bench science, and backgrounds that collectively know a great deal about defense procurement and almost nothing about industrial adhesives. Faculty asked each team to rank three finalist companies for the pre-trip briefing report — Bumrungrad International Hospital, Banpu PLC, and Avery Dennison — and we landed on Avery Dennison for the Vietnam leg, a packaging and materials manufacturer, due at six o'clock Pacific time on October 9th. A teammate drafted the body of the report; my job was to review it and add an angle the first draft didn't have.

The angle I kept returning to was this: Abbott's administrative distance into Vietnam is a story about registration pathways and reimbursement bureaucracy, the kind of friction a pharmaceutical company has budgeted for because every market makes them clear the same kind of hurdle. Avery Dennison's distance is different in kind, not just degree — a materials manufacturer's regulatory exposure in Vietnam runs through trade compliance and industrial permitting rather than anything resembling a drug approval process, which means the CAGE framework doesn't just get a new company plugged into the same four boxes. The boxes themselves measure different things depending on what you're selling. I don't think our first draft fully reckoned with that, and I said so, which is the closest thing to a contribution I had to offer a team that collectively outranks me on manufacturing know-how.

The report went in at 5:51 PM, nine minutes to spare, which is either a statement about EMBA time management or an indictment of it, depending on who in the group you ask. What it bought us was permission to get on the plane.

As I write this, that plane is close enough to feel like the actual subject of everything else — the deck, the report, the Saturday arguments about distance — rather than the reward for finishing them. A couple of weeks ago I wrote that I'd find out whether a year of discomfort built anything durable once I was standing in a market instead of describing one in a slide. I'm about to find out whether that was true, or whether it was just a tidy sentence to end a different post on.

Dan Catlin
