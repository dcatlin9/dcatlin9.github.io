## Why I Am Exploring ETA

As a Senior Scientist with a biochemistry PhD undergoing an Executive MBA, the conventional career paths usually branch in two directions: remain in corporate R&amp;D leadership, or pivot entirely into advisory or finance. 

Through my EMBA coursework—underwriting leveraged buyouts, analyzing capital structures, and studying corporate divestitures—a third path emerged that resonated far more deeply: **Entrepreneurship Through Acquisition (ETA)**.

Rather than starting a venture from scratch with binary scientific risk, ETA focuses on identifying, acquiring, and operating an established, profitable small-to-midsize company. It is an operator-first model that requires general management, financial discipline, customer empathy, and a long-term commitment to a single business.

I am actively researching and evaluating this space, developing an acquisition thesis centered on **Life Science Tools, Reagents, and Specialized Laboratory Services**.

---

## Why Life Science Tools? (The Emerging Thesis)

Niche life science tools and reagent businesses possess economic and operational characteristics that align uniquely with an operator holding both scientific depth and commercial training:

* **Sticky, Embedded Workflows:** Reagents, specialized assays, and analytical instruments often become locked into customer standard operating procedures (SOPs), academic protocols, or validated clinical workflows. Switching costs are high because reproducibility matters more than small price differences.
* **Mission-Critical Demand:** Unlike speculative drug discovery, life science tools and consumables serve the daily operational infrastructure of biopharma R&amp;D, academic core facilities, and diagnostic labs.
* **The Domain Chasm:** Many of these companies were founded by brilliant scientists or engineers who are now contemplating retirement or succession. Traditional private equity buyers often struggle to evaluate these assets because they cannot read an assay validation report, speak the language of the bench staff, or understand lab friction. 

An owner-operator who understands the science, respects the technical craft, and brings modern general management frameworks offers a natural succession partner for founders who care about what happens to their team and reputation.

## The Skills I Bring to the Operator Table

Operating a technical business requires a balance of scientific verification and financial discipline. My background spans both sides of that equation:

### 1. Scientific Depth &amp; Laboratory Empathy
* **10 Years at the Bench:** A decade solving protein structures, designing enzyme kinetics assays, and scaling laboratory workflows.
* **Technical Due Diligence Instincts:** Knowing how to interrogate assay reproducibility, lot-to-lot consistency, and customer workflow bottlenecks—not just looking at sales figures.
* **Respect for Technical Teams:** Understanding what lab technicians, formulation chemists, and QA scientists need to succeed and do their best work.

### 2. Commercial &amp; Financial Acumen (EMBA)
* **Quantitative Financial Modeling:** Hands-on experience with discounted cash flow models, LBO underwriting, capital structure evaluation, and WACC sensitivity analysis.
* **Market Entry &amp; Strategic Pricing:** Structuring razor/razorblade pricing, analyzing gross margin health, and evaluating distribution channel economics.
* **The Skeptical Generalist:** Applying the same discipline used to question an enzyme assay hypothesis to interrogate financial statement assumptions: *"What is actually being measured here?"*
